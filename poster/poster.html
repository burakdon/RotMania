01The question

Can a skill-based board game help anyone recognize how engagement-optimizing AI causes harm, and how people push back, without it feeling like a lecture? And of course while still being fun and enjoyable!

Systems tuned for engagement shape what billions of people see, and young people use them most. Ideas like specification gaming and red-teaming rarely reach the people most affected.

“Italian brainrot”, a meme genre of absurd AI-made characters, is itself a product of generative AI and recommendation feeds. Its audience already knows the look and finds it funny. That makes it a not-so-innocent vehicle.

02The story

The Berry That Came Back, an 8-page comic. A toy strawberry thrown from a car is buried under a data center and soaks up everything that leaks through the floor. What climbs out runs the city by 2048.

42 brainrots, 6 generals, 1 Darth Berry

All in-game art is procedural 3D built in code, inked by a custom WebGL renderer. Underneath: a seeded rules engine, bots, a 13-page rulebook and the backstory comic.

04Design decisions

  • Fun first, quiet lesson. It has to hold up as a euro game for someone who never reads the comic. Bogost argues games persuade through their rules; this one keeps its rules neutral, so it never feels like a quiz.
  • Skill over luck. No character select, so everyone starts equal. Secret objectives differ, and worker placement means reading and blocking the table.
  • Noir with selective color. A black-and-white ink city where only agents, brainrots and Darth Berry get color, so the eye finds what matters.
  • A villain with a wound. Darth Berry was thrown away, then fed garbage. Blame lands on incentives and training data, not on “evil AI”.
  • Borrow the look, not the characters. The brainrot style is borrowed; every character is original. No real meme character, brand or person.

06Limits & risks

  • It uses the hooks it critiques. Collectible characters and “one more turn” are engagement tools too. The game has a hard stop after six nights, and no accounts, streaks, ads or data collection.
  • A person-shaped metaphor. Darth Berry wants revenge. Real systems don’t want anything; they optimize the objective they’re given.
  • Satire can punch down. Jokes about kids and screens can turn into blaming kids. The comic points at the system and the adults who set the rules; young playtesters will tell me if that holds.
  • Contested evidence. Social media’s effect on youth mental health is debated. The game makes no scientific claim.
  • Generated art about generated content. The comic panels come from an image model with unknown training data. They’re disclosed, every prompt is published, and nothing inside the game is generated.
  • Untested on people. Balance comes from bots, not human players, and the 3D board has no screen-reader view yet.

08Playtest protocol

Who
8–12 players in groups of 3, including people outside the course.
How
Read the comic (4 min), play one game (30–45 min), short survey and a 5-minute interview.
Measure
Enjoyment (GEQ items, 1–5) · “The game was trying to teach me something” (1–5) · explain reward hacking, red-teaming, recommender in own words, before and after · “What is the story about?”
Pass if
Enjoyment median ≥ 4, preachiness median ≤ 2, and at least half link the story to engagement-optimizing AI unprompted.
